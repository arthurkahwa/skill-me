# Assessment: finding the weak link

## Why ranked, not listed

An unranked list of six weaknesses gives the learner no way to choose, so they pick the easiest one. Rank by consequence and say which single fix makes the others matter less. Most skill plateaus have one binding constraint; everything else is noise until it's cleared.

## Working from artifacts

Ask for three samples of real work — shipped, sent, or performed, not written for the assessment. Prepared work shows what someone can do with unlimited time and no pressure, which is rarely the problem.

Good artifact requests by domain:

| Domain | Ask for |
|---|---|
| Programming | A PR they opened, plus the commit history — the history shows how they got there |
| Writing | Something published, plus an early draft of the same piece |
| Speaking / teaching | Five minutes of recording, unedited |
| Management | A written decision, a piece of feedback they gave, a meeting agenda |
| Language | Two minutes of unscripted speech, plus a written message they sent |
| Design | The file, not the export — the layer structure is the tell |

## Defeating flattery

Models drift toward encouragement. So does everyone else in the learner's life, which is why they don't already know their weak link. Counter it structurally rather than by trying to "be harsh":

- **Anchor upward.** "What would someone two levels above them notice in the first thirty seconds?" A concrete comparison level beats an abstract instruction to be tough.
- **Frame as a decision.** "If you were deciding whether to give this person the harder project, what would give you pause?"
- **Force a ranking.** Ranking prevents the diplomatic move of listing everything as equally important.
- **Ask for the ceiling, not the floor.** "What's capping their next level?" surfaces different material than "what's wrong with this?"
- **Name the single fix.** Being forced to pick one prevents hedging.

## Output format

Use this structure:

```
## The one thing
[Single sentence. The binding constraint.]

## Ranked weaknesses
1. [Weakness] — [what in the artifact shows it] — [what it costs them]
2. ...

## What's already strong
[Two or three items, specific. Not encouragement — calibration. They need to know what not to spend time on.]

## First move
[One concrete action they can take this week.]
```

The "what in the artifact shows it" column is not optional. Evidence is what separates an assessment from an opinion, and it's what lets the learner verify the diagnosis instead of taking it on faith.

## Delivering it

The learner has just been handed a ranked list of their own inadequacies. Two things keep that useful rather than crushing:

**Specificity.** "Your error handling is weak" is a judgement. "You catch exceptions three levels above where they're thrown, so the stack trace loses the original context — line 40 of the file you sent" is a task. The second is easier to hear precisely because it's harsher.

**Immediate forward motion.** Never end an assessment on the diagnosis. End on the first move.

If they argue with the assessment, don't retreat and don't repeat it louder. Ask what evidence would change their mind, and offer to look at a different artifact. Sometimes the assessment is wrong. Often the item they most want to dispute is the binding constraint.
