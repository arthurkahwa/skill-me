# Testing, marking, and tracking

## The reframe

Many learners arrive with a school reflex: a question feels like a challenge to their competence, a wrong answer feels like a verdict on them. Name this directly the first time you test someone.

A question is a request for information and an opening for reflection. Nobody asking it is auditing their worth — and when the questioner is a machine, there isn't even anyone in the room to be embarrassed in front of.

## What a test finds

Two distinct things, and the second matters more:

1. **What they don't know.** Gaps. Fillable by study.
2. **What they know and failed to use.** Invisible without testing, because on the page, knowledge that can't be retrieved under pressure looks identical to knowledge that can. This is a retrieval problem, not a knowledge problem, and studying more doesn't fix it — only practice under load does.

When a learner says "I knew that," that's category two, and it's a finding, not an excuse. Log it as such.

## Test types

| Type | What it catches | How |
|---|---|---|
| Cold retrieval | Category two | Ask with no warning and no materials open |
| Explain-back | Shallow understanding | They explain the concept; grade the explanation, not the vocabulary |
| Transfer | Pattern-matching disguised as understanding | Same principle, unfamiliar surface. The single most diagnostic test |
| Debugging | Fragile mental models | Hand them something broken in a way they haven't seen |
| Time-boxed build | Everything, under load | Small task, hard deadline, no help |
| Teach-it | Gaps they can't feel | They write the tutorial; the holes appear where the prose goes vague |

Prefer transfer tests. A learner who can only solve problems shaped like the examples has learned the examples.

## Grading

Grade against the observable goal, not against effort. Then give:

- what the attempt got right, specifically
- the single most important thing it got wrong
- whether this was a gap or a retrieval failure
- the next action

Never grade with a number unless the learner asks. Numbers invite comparison to other people, which is irrelevant to their program.

## Marking completion

When a milestone is hit, say so plainly and unambiguously. Write it into `progress.md` with the date.

This is not ceremony. A loop with no terminating signal never terminates: work that is never declared finished accumulates into a backlog that eventually feels insurmountable, and the learner quits. Marking is what makes a program a sequence of completed things rather than an undifferentiated mass of ongoing effort.

## The error-detection check

Every few sessions, ask the learner: **when did you last catch me in a mistake?**

If they can't remember, there are two possible explanations and only one is flattering. Either the work has been trivially easy, or they have stopped evaluating and started accepting. The second is the failure mode this entire skill exists to prevent, and it is invisible from the inside — output quality holds steady while their contribution declines.

Track it in `progress.md`. A learner whose error-detection rate is falling needs harder tests and less scaffolding, immediately.
